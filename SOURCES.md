# The Reading Room: sources

## Texts

The library holds 256 books. The Black canon, 136 of them, fills the reading room's golden wall and the gilt cases beside the fire: the slave narratives, told by people who were enslaved or written down from their own telling; ten volumes of the WPA interviews with people born in slavery; the radical tradition; Africa and the diaspora, Black history the world over; and the speeches, essays, histories, fiction and poetry of Black writers, with four books by white witnesses and allies of abolition. Scripture and the spirit (32) stand by the fire, useful knowledge and natural history (21) on the south wall, and the great books of the world (67, among them the African and Afro-descended classics of Terence, Apuleius, Augustine, Pushkin and the two Dumas) in the Great Hall.

All 256 are in the public domain in the United States: each was published before 1931 (works first published in 1930 entered the public domain on 1 January 2026), and the WPA interviews are a work of the U.S. government. Where a book is a translation, it is the public-domain translation in the Gutenberg edition, named in the list.

Every text comes from the Project Gutenberg plain-text edition of the book, taken through the GITenberg mirrors of those editions on GitHub ([github.com/GITenberg](https://github.com/GITenberg)). For the first 26 narratives, each entry gives the year of the edition the text was taken from; for the others, the year the work was first published (for the oldest, its approximate date). Each entry gives the Gutenberg ebook number. The texts load when a reader first takes a book down or opens it, and the arcade keeps each one on the device from then on.

### How the texts were prepared

- Gutenberg's header, footer and license were removed, as its license permits for public-domain texts. So were the e-text credits, the transcribers' notes about how each e-text was made, and a short biography of Douglass that the e-text of his *Narrative* adds.
- Each book's title page is not shown, because the room displays the title and author itself. The books' own tables of contents and lists of illustrations were also dropped, because the reader builds its own.
- `--` is set as an em dash (—). Line breaks inside paragraphs were joined. Page numbers in the e-text were removed.
- Footnotes are kept as notes placed after the paragraph that refers to them.
- Pictures are not reproduced. A few of the books' own captions are kept as notes.
- Nothing else in the wording was changed. The spelling, punctuation and dialect are the authors' own.
- Tables are set one row per line. Where the columns need marking, `|` stands between the cells and `—` in an empty one.
- Where a transcriber wrote a printed symbol as a code, the symbol is used: ☞ and ☜ for the pointing hands (`[Hand->]`), ĕ and ŏ, Ɐ.
- In the books added after the first 26, a chapter longer than 20,000 words is divided for the reader into parts of about 12,000 words, cut before a heading or a paragraph and marked "(1 of 3)" and so on. Among the chapters divided this way are Montaigne's *Apology for Raimond Sebond*, four chapters of *The Wealth of Nations* and six episodes of *Ulysses*; a few books of the second expansion are divided at their own breaks instead (the Bible at chapter starts, "Genesis 1–16"), as their notes below say.
- In the first 26 narratives there are four small changes of layout:
  - In the Sojourner Truth e-text, five paragraphs break off in mid-sentence. They were joined again.
  - In Lunsford Lane's book, the poem "The Slave Mother's Address to Her Infant Child" is printed partly at the front and partly at the back of the e-text. Here it is joined into one piece at the end.
  - In Noah Davis's book, the Sabbath School table prints its column heads as vertical letters. Here it is set as rows, with `|` between the cells and `—` in the empty ones.
  - Publishers' advertisements at the back of Pennington's and Davis's books were left out.

  - the transcribers' bracketed notes that guess at a spelling or describe the e-text;

Tolstoy's "Lesson of *The Kreutzer Sonata*" is kept as a chapter, and the subtitle of "Polikushka" is a heading in its Chapter I.
- ***Pudd'nhead Wilson*.** The e-text is the Hartford edition of 1894. Its chapter titles, which its transcriber says were not part of Twain's book and come from another version, were dropped, so the chapters are numbered only. The transcriber's long notes at the end were dropped.
- ***Heart of Darkness*.** Gutenberg has two differing e-texts of the story; this is #219. Its three sections were titled Part I, Part II and Part III. The blurb names Chinua Achebe's critique of 1975.
- **Sophocles.** Storr's translation is from the Loeb Classical Library (1912). Each play has an Argument chapter, a Dramatis Personae chapter and one chapter for its whole text, since the plays have no scene divisions and run 12,000 to 18,000 words. The Loeb credit page of each play was dropped. Stage directions ("[Enter …]") were set as prose between the speeches, and the starred lines that mark lost Greek text are kept.
- **Terence.** The e-text is Harper's reprint (1896) of Colman's verse translation; where Harper's text was clearly wrong, the transcriber substituted the reading of Colman's second edition (1768). The transcriber's 95 blocks of variant readings ("[Changes: …]", "[Erratum: …]") at the ends of scenes were dropped. Each act was made a chapter ("The Andrian, Act I"), with its scenes, often a few speeches long, as headings; each play's persons and prologue are chapters of their own, and each speech is a speaker heading followed by verse. The transcriber's six stage directions in braces ("{_CLINIA and SYRUS apart._}"), which stand for passages printed side by side, are kept. Colman's epigraph from Horace was kept as a short chapter. As printed, "PROLOGUE." appears among the persons.
- ***Romeo and Juliet* and *Julius Caesar*.** These were set like the other Shakespeare plays: one chapter per scene, with speakers' names as printed. A prologue spoken before a scene was made a chapter of its own ("The Prologue", "Act II, Prologue"). A one-line speech is set as verse or as prose to match the speeches around it, so short prose exchanges are not always set consistently.
- ***Faust*.** The e-text has Part One only. This edition, illustrated by Harry Clarke, prints its section titles only as picture captions ("[Illustration: =Dedication=]"); these were made the chapter titles. The scenes were named from the book's contents ("Scene III — The Study (The Exorcism)"), with their printed titles kept as headings. All the speech is verse, except the prose scene "Dreary Day". In Taylor's preface, the notes A to K, which the e-text already sets after their paragraphs, were set as notes, each with the quotations it contains; note I is labelled "I:" in the e-text. Taylor's German poem "An Goethe" is kept.
- **Confucius.** The translation is from Legge's *Chinese Classics*, vol. 1 (1861), without his notes. The e-text keeps the printed page breaks as blank lines, which fall inside paragraphs, so a paragraph was taken to begin only at an indented line or a "CHAP." or "BOOK" line; two words divided across a page keep their hyphen ("adminis-trative"). Each Book was made a chapter, titled with its Chinese name ("Book I — Hsio R").
- **Plato, *Apology, Crito and Phædo*.** Note 33's marker is misprinted as a second "[32]"; notes 32 and 33 were placed after their paragraphs by hand, and the text is unchanged. The Phædo (30,000 words) was divided into three parts at its sections 61 and 102.
- **Aristotle.** The e-text is Chase's translation in the Everyman edition, with J. A. Smith's introduction, from a later printing: its bibliography after the introduction lists works up to 1933, so it was left out; Smith's 1911 introduction and Chase's translation are kept. Each Book was made a chapter; the chapter numerals standing alone were set as headings, and the "[Sidenote: …]" markers (chapter numbers and Bekker pages) are kept in the text. Chase's appendix after Book VI and his Notes, keyed to pages, are chapters of their own. One diagram caption was dropped.
- **Epictetus.** The e-text is a selection from Long's translation (1877), with his biographical note. Each discourse opens with its title in capitals run into the first paragraph; the title was made the chapter title. A note on one title was placed at the head of its chapter. The heading "A Selection from the Discourses of Epictetus" heads the first discourse, and the numbered sections of the Encheiridion are headings.
- **Boethius.** The e-text is H. R. James's translation (1897). The title-page mottoes (from Aristotle's *Ethics* in Greek, transliterated in the e-text, and two sentences of Boethius in Latin) were kept as an "Epigraphs" chapter; the frontispiece caption and the index of verse interludes were dropped. Each Book was made a chapter. Note [L], printed as a bare "[L]" and three short paragraphs, was made one note. The References to Quotations are kept, with the page numbers of 1897.
- **Pascal.** Kegan Paul's translation (1885) of Molinier's text and arrangement was used, because Gutenberg's text of Trotter's translation (#18269) is a 1958 printing with T. S. Eliot's introduction of 1931. Molinier's section titles are the chapters, and the titles of the two Parts are headings. The repeated half-titles and the index were dropped. The Notes, keyed to the pages of the 1885 edition, are kept as printed.
- **Paine.** The e-text is the Bradford edition of February 1776, with the Appendix and the address to the Quakers. The title-page epigraph, from Thomson, was kept as a short chapter. Dropped: the transcriber's quotations "On Common Sense", the transcriber's notes, and the gloss "[pounds sterling]" after £ in the two tables of the cost of a navy. Those tables are set one row per line, without column marks.
- **Mill, *On Liberty*.** The e-text is the Walter Scott edition of 1901, with W. L. Courtney's introduction, whose sections I–III are headings. The Humboldt epigraph was kept as a short chapter. The notes, printed in "Footnotes" sections, were placed after the paragraphs that cite them, with note 6's second paragraph kept with it.
- **William James.** The e-text is the Gifford Lectures as published in 1902, in a printing of 1917. The 363 notes, printed at the end, were placed after the paragraphs (or verse) that cite them; their markers stay as printed ("(1)"), and the notes are numbered "[1]". The rules of hyphens are section breaks. Lectures XI–XIII (Saintliness), printed as one chapter of 24,000 words, were divided in two at a section break, the second part titled "(Continued)". The appendix to Lectures IV and V stays where it is printed. The index was dropped.

*Useful knowledge and natural history*

- **Walton.** The e-text is the facsimile reprint (1897) of the first edition of 1653, with Richard Le Gallienne's preface. The 1653 title page and the box drawn around the speakers' names were dropped. Chapter I has no heading in the book and was titled "Chapter I".
- **Franklin.** The e-text is Frank Woodworth Pine's edition of 1916; the chapter titles, notes, bibliography and appendix are his. The facsimile of two *Poor Richard* almanac pages, the Father Abraham title page and the transcriber's transcriptions of newspaper pages were dropped as pictures. The chart of the virtues and the scheme of the day were set as rows, with `|` between the cells, and the chart's own heading rows, which repeat the three lines above it, were folded into those lines. Pine's notes, printed indented, were placed after the paragraphs that cite them.
- **Brillat-Savarin.** The e-text is Fayette Robinson's translation, which leaves out three meditations, a chapter and a few passages; his notes saying so are kept. The 39 notes printed inside the text ("[Footnote: …]") were taken out of their sentences and set after the paragraph as [n], with a marker left in place; two notes printed on a meditation's title open that chapter instead. "Meditation First" was numbered "Meditation I", and the sections of the second part were titled "Part Second — Transition" and "Part Second — Varieties".
- **Smiles.** The e-text is the popular edition of 1897. The note markers `{n}` were set as [n], with the notes after their paragraphs. Chapter III's misprinted heading, "HE GREAT POTTERS", takes the wording of the contents, "Three Great Potters". The title-page mottoes were made an "Epigraphs" chapter.
- **Nightingale.** The e-text is the first edition, published by Harrison, not the reprint of 1898. Each sidenote ("[Sidenote: …]") was set as a heading, or, inside a note, as the note's first line. The endnotes, some several paragraphs long, were placed after the paragraphs that cite them, and a transcriber's bracketed guess inside note [3] was removed. Tables A and B were set as rows, with `|` between the cells and their column heads read downward. The publisher's advertisements were dropped.
- **Beeton.** The epigraph from Milton was kept as a short first chapter. The analytical index, the contents and 567 illustration markers were dropped. Chapter titles come from the book's General Contents; where it calls a chapter only "Recipes", the subject was added in brackets ("Chapter XIII — Recipes (Beef)"). A heading that repeats its chapter's title was dropped. Eight chapters over 20,000 words were divided into parts ("(1 of 3)" and so on): seven recipe chapters, each cut before a recipe, and "Domestic Servants". The tables, among them bills of fare and table plans, were set as rows, with `|` between the cells; some table heads are divided into cells only approximately. A few recipe titles that run straight into an unnumbered paragraph are left as plain paragraphs.
- **Washington, *Character Building*.** The note on the title "Education That Educates" opens that chapter.
- **Washington, *Working with the Hands*.** The documents printed in the text (a contract, menus, a programme, a pledge and a report form) are kept. The photograph captions were dropped, among them that of a planting plan in Chapter XII, which is a picture.
- **Washington (ed.), *Tuskegee & Its People*.** The chapters were titled "Part I, Chapter I — …" and so on; each part's heading opens its first chapter, and the "BY …" lines are kept as headings.
- **Estes.** Each recipe name, which the e-text runs into its recipe (`~ASPARAGUS SOUP~--`), was set as a heading, with the recipe as a paragraph after it (594 recipes); one name with a qualifier outside the marks, `~VEGETABLE SOUP~ (without stock)`, was made one heading. The blank "Additional Recipes" pages and the index were dropped.
- **King.** The tables were set as rows, with `|` between the cells; some table heads are divided into cells only approximately. The figures the text cites are not in the e-text.
- **Hackley.** The chapter heading "Her Relationship With Men" was set as "Her Relationship with Men".
- **White.** The e-text (#1408) has the letters only, without *The Antiquities of Selborne*. White's notes, run into the text as "(* …)", were set as notes. The misprint "T Thomas Pennant" was corrected to "To", and two letter headings split in the e-text were joined. The letters were titled "To Thomas Pennant — Letter I", "To Daines Barrington — Letter I" and so on. The five poems at the front of the e-text were made a chapter, "Poems".
- **Faraday.** The misprinted marker [14] in Lecture V was corrected to [16], the note it belongs to. The e-text's two small diagrams drawn in characters (of water, and of sugar with a bracket) were set as short rows. The editor's notes were placed after the paragraphs that cite them.
- **Darwin, *The Formation of Vegetable Mould*.** The note markers `{n}` and `{4a}` were set as [n]. The five tables were set one row per entry, with `|` between the cells. The figure captions are kept as notes ("Illustration: …"), because the text cites the figures.
- **Darwin, *Autobiography*.** This is the text Francis Darwin printed in *The Life and Letters of Charles Darwin* (1887), which leaves out passages restored in 1958. His section headings were used as chapter titles, and the untitled first section was titled "Recollections of the Development of My Mind and Character", the autobiography's own heading.

## The room

- **The epigraph** on the title screen is from Douglass's *Narrative*, Chapter VI, checked against the text in `books/douglass-narrative.json`: "From that moment, I understood the pathway from slavery to freedom."
- **The Sankofa** over the mantel is the arcade's own vetted drawing (the heart form, `adinkra/index.html`), whose sources are in `adinkra/SOURCES.md`. It is reused path for path, in gold leaf on oxblood.
- **The frieze** carries the narrators' names (the subject's name, Tubman, for Bradford's book), the way older reading rooms carve their authors' names.
- **The shelves.** The reading room holds the Black canon. The east wall is the golden wall: at its centre the narratives, under the WPA interviews; to one side Africa and the diaspora (Black history the world over, including older and contested works on Egypt and Ethiopia, whose blurbs say plainly what they claim) and the radical tradition; to the other the speeches and essays and the historians. Its cases are backed in an original damask pattern, edged in gilt and lit by brass picture lights. On the north wall, beside the fire, are the fiction, the poets and the witnesses (also gilt), and scripture and the spirit; on the south wall, useful knowledge and natural history. The great books of the world fill the Great Hall. Each section in the reading room has a plaque. The spines are the arcade's own designs, not copies of particular bindings: cloth with gilt rules for the Black canon, and leather with raised bands and a title label for the rest. The plain volumes filling out the shelves are scenery, not books you can read. Books are drawn a little larger than life, so a spine can be read from across the room.
- **Begin here.** A gilt-edged runner leads from the middle of the room to the narratives' shelves, a warm light falls on them, their books glow softly where they stand, and their plaque says to begin there; first-time readers are pointed to them.
- **Who you are.** The books of the person you read as glow for you alone: their own books; for Harriet Bailey, her son's *Narrative* and *My Bondage and My Freedom*, where she is remembered (her description, her twelve-mile walks at night, and that she could read are Douglass's words in *My Bondage and My Freedom*); for Malcolm X, books he names from his reading in the Norfolk Prison Colony in *The Autobiography of Malcolm X* (1965), chapter "Saved": Du Bois's *The Souls of Black Folk*, *Uncle Tom's Cabin* (he says it was the only novel he read once his serious reading began), Fanny Kemble's journal, the Confessions of Nat Turner, Herodotus, and Woodson's *The Negro in Our History*, where they are on the shelves. Malcolm X is chosen once you have taken down ten books and read one to the end.
- **The shared library.** On the claude.ai page everyone who opens it walks into one library. Where each reader stands travels as their live presence, and changes to the room as live messages, through claude.ai's room capability; the library itself, and the margin notes readers share, are kept in the page's shared database, so they are still there after everyone leaves. Nothing else about a reader is sent: no account details, only the name they type and the figure they choose. On the library's own website (library.jordanstrudwick.com, built by `deploy/site/build.js`) the same shared library runs over three public Nostr relays (relay.damus.io, nos.lol, relay.primal.net), through `deploy/site/commons-nostr.js` and nostr-tools 2.25.2 (Unlicense; its browser bundle includes Paul Miller's MIT-licensed noble and scure libraries, whose licence ships beside it). There, each browser signs with a random key of its own, and the library's layout and shared notes are public on those relays.
- **The Great Hall** lies through the double doors on the south wall. It is a domed rotunda in the manner of the great reading rooms: a ring of oak bookcases with a balcony tier above, a drum of windows, and a glass dome over a night sky. The compass rose in its floor points north, back to the Reading Room. Its shelves hold the great books of the world: the novel, epic and drama, and philosophy and politics. The doors open when you finish your first chapter, and stay open.
- **The Banned Books gallery**, off the hall's east side, opens when you finish your first whole book. Its ten vitrines each hold a book from this collection that was banned, burned, seized or pulled from the shelves somewhere. The plaques say what happened, and nothing more:
  - *Walker's Appeal*: Georgia's capital law of December 1829 against circulating insurrectionary writing, and North Carolina's two laws of 1830, against seditious publications and against teaching enslaved people to read. Sources: Zinn Education Project, "Georgia Bans Insurrectionary Literature" <https://www.zinnedproject.org/news/tdih/georgia-bans-insurrectionary-literature/>; Equal Justice Initiative, 15 November 1830 <https://calendar.eji.org/racial-injustice/nov/15>; Leventhal Map & Education Center <https://www.leventhalmap.org/digital-exhibitions/declarations/walkers-appeal/>.
  - *Uncle Tom's Cabin*: Samuel Green's ten-year sentence in Maryland in 1857, and his pardon in 1862. Sources: Archives of Maryland, biography of Samuel Green <https://msa.maryland.gov/megafile/msa/speccol/sc5400/sc5496/051300/051332/html/51332bio.html>; House Divided Project, Dickinson College <https://stampedes.dickinson.edu/node/1020>.
  - *Southern Horrors*: the mob that wrecked the *Free Speech* in Memphis, 27 May 1892. Sources: Equal Justice Initiative, 27 May 1892 <https://calendar.eji.org/racial-injustice/may/27>; National Constitution Center <https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/constitutional-voices-ida-b-wells>. That the pamphlet grew out of her *New York Age* account of 25 June 1892 is from its own preface, in this collection.
  - *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn*: the Concord library committee, March 1885, and Twain's words about it. Source: *Mark Twain in His Times*, University of Virginia <https://twain.lib.virginia.edu/huckfinn/hfconcrd.html>.
  - *Leaves of Grass*: Boston, 1882. Sources: The Walt Whitman Archive <https://whitmanarchive.org/item/encyclopedia_entry661>; the Lilly Library, Indiana University <https://collections.libraries.indiana.edu/lilly/exhibitions/exhibits/show/whitmanlilly/mask/suppressed>.
  - *Ulysses*: the 1921 *Little Review* ruling and Judge Woolsey's of December 1933. Sources: The Free Speech Center, Middle Tennessee State University <https://firstamendment.mtsu.edu/article/ulysses/>; Banned Books, Indiana University <https://bannedbooks.indiana.edu/items/show/11>.
  - *The Prince*, Montaigne's *Essays* and *Les Misérables*: the Catholic Church's Index of Forbidden Books (1559, 1676, 1864), abolished in 1966. Sources: University of Toronto Libraries, "Machiavelli's *The Prince* in the Sixteenth Century" <https://spatial-humanities.library.utoronto.ca/exhibits/show/seventeenth-century-machiavel/prince-introduction>; Larousse, *Essais* <https://www.larousse.fr/encyclopedie/oeuvre/Essais/118474>; the Index's entry for Hugo, as given in a study published on Fabula <https://www.fabula.org/actualites/documents/90829_2.pdf>; Fordham University's Internet History Sourcebooks, on the Index <https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/mod/indexlibrorum.asp>.
  - *The Communist Manifesto*: the book burnings of 10 May 1933. Sources: Anne Frank House <https://www.annefrank.org/en/timeline/145/book-burning-at-german-universities/>; Jewish Virtual Library <https://www.jewishvirtuallibrary.org/the-burning-of-the-books>.
- **Zooming in** narrows the field of view, like a longer lens, up to 6×, so the spines can be read from across the room.
- **The celestial globe** shows the Big Dipper pointing to Polaris. Star positions are J2000 right ascension and declination, rounded (Dubhe 11h03.7 +61.75, Merak 11h01.8 +56.38, Phecda 11h53.8 +53.69, Megrez 12h15.4 +57.03, Alioth 12h54.0 +55.96, Mizar 13h23.9 +54.93, Alkaid 13h47.5 +49.31, Polaris 2h31.8 +89.26). Its note quotes Henry Bibb's *Narrative*, Chapter IV ("guided by the unchangable North Star by night", his spelling). The North Star as the guide north is named in these books again and again: Bibb, W. W. Brown, Douglass (both books), Jacobs, Delaney, Steward, J. D. Green and Bradford's *Harriet*. The globe is labelled "The Big Dipper", not "The Drinking Gourd": the song that gives that name was first published in 1928, and its Underground Railroad origin is disputed, so it is left out.
- **The rugs** are original geometric designs in Kuba-cloth colours (raffia, rust, black). They are not copies of particular cloths and make no claim about any pattern's meaning.
- **Reading aloud.** The speaker button in the reader reads any book aloud, for anyone. Earlier versions of the room let you walk in as Sojourner Truth or Harriet Tubman, who never learned to read or write, and read books aloud to them; their places among the readers are now Harriet Bailey's and Phillis Wheatley's. The *Narrative of Sojourner Truth* and Bradford's *Harriet* stay on the shelves.
- **The readers** you can choose are stylised figures, not portraits. Each is drawn from the clothes and hair in widely published photographs and engravings: Douglass's white swept-back hair and full beard in his later portraits, with frock coat and bow tie; Phillis Wheatley's mob cap, dark gown and white collar in the 1773 frontispiece of her *Poems*; Harriet Jacobs's parted, drawn-back hair and dark high-collared dress in her 1894 photograph; Ida B. Wells's upswept hair, high collar and brooch; Booker T. Washington's close crop, dark three-piece suit and high collar; Du Bois's goatee, three-piece suit and watch chain; and Malcolm X's browline glasses and narrow tie. No likeness of Harriet Bailey, Douglass's mother, survives. She is built from his words in *My Bondage and My Freedom*: "tall, and finely proportioned; of deep black, glossy complexion; had regular features", and "remarkably sedate in her manners". She is dressed as a Maryland field hand of the 1820s, in tow linen, an apron and a headwrap. Douglass learned after her death that she could read, "the only one of all the slaves and colored people in Tuckahoe who enjoyed that advantage". Heights are approximate: Douglass at about six feet, Du Bois at five foot six and Malcolm X at six foot three are well attested; the others are estimates.
- **Code.** three.js r158 (`../lib/three.min.js`, shared across the arcade). PeerJS 1.5.5 (MIT), vendored unmodified as `vendor/peerjs.min.js` and loaded only when someone opens or joins a shared room. Rooms use PeerJS's public matchmaking server (0.peerjs.com) to exchange a room code, then connect browser to browser with public STUN servers (Google, Twilio).
- **Sound** is synthesised in the browser: the fire, footsteps, pages and the furniture. Read-aloud uses the voices built into your device (the Web Speech API).


## The cinema

A roll-up screen hangs on the chimney breast, in front of the Sankofa, and a projector rises out of a walnut stand behind the sofa (`cinema.js`). Its programme is race films (films made for Black audiences, most of them by Black filmmakers) and other early Black cinema in the U.S. public domain. U.S. copyright on works published before 1931 has run out; the two later films are free for the reasons given with them. The notes in the programme are short and factual.

Every film plays from Wikimedia Commons, whose file server (upload.wikimedia.org) sends CORS headers, so the picture can be painted onto the 3D screen. A file's address there is fixed by its name: MediaWiki files it under the first one and first two hex digits of the MD5 of the name (spaces as underscores). Where Commons holds only a 1080p master of several gigabytes, the room first asks for the 480p VP9 copy Commons makes of every video (`…/transcoded/<h>/<hh>/<name>/<name>.480p.vp9.webm`) and falls back to the master, or to a smaller upload of the same film. The file names below were confirmed from the Commons file pages as they appear in search results; the room was built where archive.org and Wikimedia could not be reached, so the files themselves were not fetched, and the 480p copies in particular are named by Commons' scheme rather than seen.

**Your own links.** The cinema also plays a link you paste. A direct video file (.mp4, .webm) plays on the screen if its server sends CORS headers, and otherwise in a window over the room. A YouTube link plays in a window, in YouTube's privacy-enhanced player (youtube-nocookie.com); the screen behind it shows a title card, since an embedded player cannot be painted onto a 3D surface. A shared room carries only the link (for YouTube, the video's id), never the video, and checks it: only http and https links, and only well-formed YouTube ids. What you choose to play is your own responsibility.
