# The Reading Room: sources

## Texts

The library holds 421 books. The Black canon, 292 of them, fills the reading room's golden wall and the gilt cases beside the fire: the slave narratives, told by people who were enslaved or written down from their own telling; thirty-four volumes of the WPA interviews with people born in slavery (every state volume of the Library of Congress's "Born in Slavery" collection, with its administrative files); the radical tradition; Africa and the diaspora, Black history the world over; and the speeches, essays, histories, fiction and poetry of Black writers, with five books by white witnesses and allies of abolition. Scripture and the spirit (41) stand by the fire, useful knowledge and natural history (21) on the south wall, and the great books of the world (67, among them the African and Afro-descended classics of Terence, Apuleius, Augustine, Pushkin and the two Dumas) in the Great Hall.

All 421 are in the public domain in the United States: each was published before 1931 (works first published in 1930 entered the public domain on 1 January 2026), and the WPA interviews are a work of the U.S. government. Where a book is a translation, it is the public-domain translation in the edition named in the list.

The first 256 texts come from Project Gutenberg's plain-text editions, taken through the GITenberg mirrors of those editions on GitHub ([github.com/GITenberg](https://github.com/GITenberg)). The 102 added in October 2026 come from Project Gutenberg itself (44), Documenting the American South at the University of North Carolina (41), English Wikisource (9), the Text Creation Partnership (3), NYPL's Digital Schomburg (2), A Celebration of Women Writers at the University of Pennsylvania, Encyclopedia Virginia and the Internet Sacred Text Archive (1 each), and 51 more in a second round from Gutenberg and Documenting the American South; each entry names its source. For the first 26 narratives, each entry gives the year of the edition the text was taken from; for the others, the year the work was first published (for the oldest, its approximate date). Each Gutenberg entry gives the ebook number. The texts load when a reader first takes a book down or opens it, and the arcade keeps each one on the device from then on.

### How the texts were prepared

- Gutenberg's header, footer and license were removed, as its license permits for public-domain texts. So were the e-text credits, the transcribers' notes about how each e-text was made, and a short biography of Douglass that the e-text of his *Narrative* adds.
- Each book's title page is not shown, because the room displays the title and author itself. The books' own tables of contents and lists of illustrations were also dropped, because the reader builds its own.
- `--` is set as an em dash (—). Line breaks inside paragraphs were joined. Page numbers in the e-text were removed.
- Footnotes are kept as notes placed after the paragraph that refers to them.
- Pictures are not reproduced. A few of the books' own captions are kept as notes.
- Nothing else in the wording was changed. The spelling, punctuation and dialect are the authors' own.
- Tables are set one row per line. Where the columns need marking, `|` stands between the cells and `—` in an empty one.
- Where a transcriber wrote a printed symbol as a code, the symbol is used: ☞ and ☜ for the pointing hands (`[Hand->]`), ĕ and ŏ, Ɐ.
- In the books added after the first 26, a chapter longer than 20,000 words is divided for the reader into parts of about 12,000 words, cut before a heading or a paragraph and marked "(1 of 3)" and so on. Among the chapters divided this way are Montaigne's *Apology for Raimond Sebond*, four chapters of *The Wealth of Nations* and six episodes of *Ulysses*; a few books of the second expansion are divided at their own breaks instead (the Bible at chapter starts, "Genesis 1–16"), as their notes below say.
- In the first 26 narratives there are four small changes of layout:
  - In the Sojourner Truth e-text, five paragraphs break off in mid-sentence. They were joined again.
  - In Lunsford Lane's book, the poem "The Slave Mother's Address to Her Infant Child" is printed partly at the front and partly at the back of the e-text. Here it is joined into one piece at the end.
  - In Noah Davis's book, the Sabbath School table prints its column heads as vertical letters. Here it is set as rows, with `|` between the cells and `—` in the empty ones.
  - Publishers' advertisements at the back of Pennington's and Davis's books were left out.

- **Sources beyond Gutenberg (October 2026).** Documenting the American South's books were taken from its TEI files, with its page markers, banners, catalogue records and notes of its own ("print not legible", shown as [?]) left out. Where it supplies a correction in brackets, supplied punctuation was taken in and supplied words were not; misprints it marks [sic] are printed as the book printed them. Where its TEI and its HTML page disagree on a word, the books' notes say which reading was taken. Wikisource's transcriptions, even those marked proofread or validated, were checked against the page scans, and the corrections are listed book by book; three Wikisource books whose texts did not match their printings were left out (Garvey's *Philosophy and Opinions*, Plaatje's *Mhudi*, Du Bois's *Dark Princess*), as were Hopkins's *Hagar's Daughter* and Maria W. Stewart's *Productions*, whose transcriptions could not be checked against a printing. The Text Creation Partnership's texts keep their old spellings; the long s is set as s. Each book was checked word for word against the text of its source with `tools/library-books/booklib.py`, whose check fails on any word added, dropped or changed.

## The room

- **The epigraph** on the title screen is from Douglass's *Narrative*, checked word for word against the text in `books/douglass-narrative.json` (the `title` browser test checks it again). The four quoted sentences are from the third paragraph of Chapter VI, cut at the ellipses: "These words sank deep into my heart …", "I now understood …", "From that moment, I understood the pathway from slavery to freedom." and "What he most dreaded, that I most desired." The words around them are ours, from the same book: Hugh and Sophia Auld and his age, "between seven and eight years old", from Chapter V; Mrs. Auld's lessons in the A, B, C and her husband's warning that reading "would forever unfit him to be a slave", from that same paragraph of Chapter VI; and the bread he gave the boys in the street for "that more valuable bread of knowledge", from Chapter VII. The slur in Hugh Auld's words is the book's; it stays in the book and is left off the title page.
- **The Sankofa** over the mantel is the arcade's own vetted drawing (the heart form, `adinkra/index.html`), whose sources are in `adinkra/SOURCES.md`. It is reused path for path, in gold leaf on oxblood.
- **The frieze** carries the narrators' names (the subject's name, Tubman, for Bradford's book), the way older reading rooms carve their authors' names.
- **The shelves.** The reading room holds the Black canon. The east wall is the golden wall: at its centre the narratives, under the WPA interviews; to one side Africa and the diaspora (Black history the world over, including older and contested works on Egypt and Ethiopia, whose blurbs say plainly what they claim) and the radical tradition; to the other the speeches and essays and the historians. Its cases are backed in an original damask pattern, edged in gilt and lit by brass picture lights. On the north wall, beside the fire, are the fiction, the poets and the witnesses (also gilt), and scripture and the spirit; on the south wall, useful knowledge and natural history. The great books of the world fill the Great Hall. Each section in the reading room has a plaque. The spines are the arcade's own designs, not copies of particular bindings: cloth with gilt rules for the Black canon, and leather with raised bands and a title label for the rest. The plain volumes filling out the shelves are scenery, not books you can read. Books are drawn a little larger than life, so a spine can be read from across the room.
- **Begin here.** A gilt-edged runner leads from the middle of the room to the narratives' shelves, a warm light falls on them, their books glow softly where they stand, and their plaque says to begin there; first-time readers are pointed to them.
- **Who you are.** The books of the person you read as glow for you alone: their own books; for Harriet Bailey, her son's *Narrative* and *My Bondage and My Freedom*, where she is remembered (her description, her twelve-mile walks at night, and that she could read are Douglass's words in *My Bondage and My Freedom*); for Paul Robeson, *Othello*, which he played in London in 1930, and the books of the spirituals, which he sang in concert from 1925.
- **The shared library.** On the claude.ai page everyone who opens it walks into one library. Where each reader stands travels as their live presence, and changes to the room as live messages, through claude.ai's room capability; the library itself, and the margin notes readers share, are kept in the page's shared database, so they are still there after everyone leaves. Nothing else about a reader is sent: no account details, only the name they type and the figure they choose. On the library's own website (library.jordanstrudwick.com, built by `deploy/site/build.js`) the same shared library runs over three public Nostr relays (relay.damus.io, nos.lol, relay.primal.net), through `deploy/site/commons-nostr.js` and nostr-tools 2.25.2 (Unlicense; its browser bundle includes Paul Miller's MIT-licensed noble and scure libraries, whose licence ships beside it). There, each browser signs with a random key of its own, and the library's layout and shared notes are public on those relays.
- **The Great Hall** lies through the double doors on the south wall. It is a domed rotunda in the manner of the great reading rooms: a ring of oak bookcases with a balcony tier above, a drum of windows, and a glass dome over a night sky. The compass rose in its floor points north, back to the Reading Room. Its shelves hold the great books of the world: the novel, epic and drama, and philosophy and politics. The doors open when you finish your first chapter, and stay open.

- **Zooming in** narrows the field of view, like a longer lens, up to 6×, so the spines can be read from across the room.
- **The celestial globe** shows the Big Dipper pointing to Polaris. Star positions are J2000 right ascension and declination, rounded (Dubhe 11h03.7 +61.75, Merak 11h01.8 +56.38, Phecda 11h53.8 +53.69, Megrez 12h15.4 +57.03, Alioth 12h54.0 +55.96, Mizar 13h23.9 +54.93, Alkaid 13h47.5 +49.31, Polaris 2h31.8 +89.26). Its note quotes Henry Bibb's *Narrative*, Chapter IV ("guided by the unchangable North Star by night", his spelling). The North Star as the guide north is named in these books again and again: Bibb, W. W. Brown, Douglass (both books), Jacobs, Delaney, Steward, J. D. Green and Bradford's *Harriet*. The globe is labelled "The Big Dipper", not "The Drinking Gourd": the song that gives that name was first published in 1928, and its Underground Railroad origin is disputed, so it is left out.
- **The rugs** are original geometric designs in Kuba-cloth colours (raffia, rust, black). They are not copies of particular cloths and make no claim about any pattern's meaning.
- **Reading aloud.** The speaker button in the reader reads any book aloud, for anyone. Earlier versions of the room let you walk in as Sojourner Truth or Harriet Tubman, who never learned to read or write, and read books aloud to them; their places among the readers are now Harriet Bailey's and Phillis Wheatley's. The *Narrative of Sojourner Truth* and Bradford's *Harriet* stay on the shelves.
- **The readers** you can choose are stylised figures, not portraits. Each is drawn from the clothes and hair in widely published photographs and engravings: Douglass's white swept-back hair and full beard in his later portraits, with frock coat and bow tie; Phillis Wheatley's mob cap, dark gown and white collar in the 1773 frontispiece of her *Poems*; Harriet Jacobs's parted, drawn-back hair and dark high-collared dress in her 1894 photograph; Ida B. Wells's upswept hair, high collar and brooch; Booker T. Washington's close crop, dark three-piece suit and high collar; Du Bois's goatee, three-piece suit and watch chain; Paul Robeson's height and breadth, receding hairline and suit; Kwame Ture twice: as the young Stokely Carmichael of 1966 to 1968, in the black turtleneck and dark glasses, and in his last years in Guinea, with grey natural hair, a short grey beard and a white West African robe embroidered at the neck and down the front (the late photograph on the Review of African Political Economy's "Becoming Kwame Ture", 2020, and a 1988 Toronto Star photograph in Digital Archive Ontario). No likeness of Harriet Bailey, Douglass's mother, survives. She is built from his words in *My Bondage and My Freedom*: "tall, and finely proportioned; of deep black, glossy complexion; had regular features", and "remarkably sedate in her manners". She is dressed as a Maryland field hand of the 1820s, in tow linen, an apron and a headwrap. Douglass learned after her death that she could read, "the only one of all the slaves and colored people in Tuckahoe who enjoyed that advantage". The readers stand at their real heights, relative to one another, where those are known: Douglass at about six feet, Du Bois at five foot six, and Paul Robeson at six foot three are well attested; Harriet Jacobs at "about 5 feet 4 inches high" is from the runaway notice James Norcom placed in the *American Beacon* in 1835 (PBS, *Africans in America* <https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/aia/part4/4h1541t.html>); King at about five foot seven is the figure most often given. Harriet Bailey is tall, in her son's words. Kwame Ture's height is not recorded; at about six foot two, both his figures are estimated from Bob Fitch's 1966 photograph of him walking beside King in the March Against Fear (Stanford University Libraries <https://purl.stanford.edu/qx527cr2135>), where he stands about an eighth taller, and agree with Peniel Joseph's description of him as tall. Ida B. Wells, Booker T. Washington and Phillis Wheatley have no recorded height and stand at about the average of their time.
- **What the new readers' notes say.** Paul Robeson (1898–1976): his father, William Drew Robeson, escaped slavery in Martin County, North Carolina, as a teenager (accounts give 1858 or 1860); Paul was valedictorian of Rutgers' class of 1919 and took his law degree at Columbia in 1923; his concert with Lawrence Brown at the Greenwich Village Theatre on 19 April 1925 was built on the spirituals; he played Othello at the Savoy Theatre, London, in 1930. Sources: Rutgers, "Paul Robeson, 1919" <https://scarletandblack.rutgers.edu/archive/exhibits/show/alumni-gallery/robeson>; Paul Robeson and William Drew Robeson I on Wikipedia <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Robeson>, <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Drew_Robeson_I>; Lawrence Benjamin Brown <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Benjamin_Brown>; University of Michigan Library, "Paul Robeson's Othello" <https://blogs.lib.umich.edu/beyond-reading-room/dignity-and-stately-magnificence-paul-robesons-othello>. Kwame Ture (1941–1998), born Stokely Carmichael in Port of Spain, Trinidad: at the Bronx High School of Science from 1956, where on his first day he started a list of writers to read "as quickly as possible" (Gotham Center for New York City History, "Stokely Carmichael: The Boy Before Black Power" <https://www.gothamcenter.org/blog/stokelycarmichael>); a Freedom Rider in 1961, at nineteen; a philosophy graduate of Howard in 1964 (Howard University, *The Dig* <https://thedig.howard.edu/featured-people/kwame-ture>); in Lowndes County, Alabama, from 1965, where the number of Black voters registered rose from 70 to 2,600; he took the name Kwame Ture to honour Kwame Nkrumah and Sékou Touré (Wikipedia, "Stokely Carmichael" <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stokely_Carmichael>). He took the name in Guinea, where he made his home and organised for the All-African People's Revolutionary Party until his death in Conakry in 1998. Both of Ture's figures, with Nkrumah and Rodney, are hidden until a reader has taken down ten books and read one to the end. His own books and those he is known to have read (Padmore, Fanon, Nkrumah) are not in the public domain, so no books glow for him yet.
- **Four more readers (November 2026).** Drawn at the moment each was most a reader and writer, from photographs
  that are free to use, except Rodney's, which no free photograph exists for: no photograph is copied.

  association's badge on his lapel (Library of Congress, Bain Collection, 5 August 1924). He is drawn in the suit,
  not in the ceremonial uniform of the August parades, which his opponents used to mock him. Kwame Nkrumah
  (1909–1972), as the "avid reader" of the 1942 Lincoln University yearbook (round spectacles, dark suit, white
  pocket square); his height, about 5 ft 9 in, is an estimate, measured against President Kennedy in two JFK

  "a slim man of average height", known by his beard and spectacles (Guyana Commission of Inquiry, 2016, vol. 1,

## The cinema

A roll-up screen hangs on the chimney breast, in front of the Sankofa, and a projector rises out of a walnut stand behind the sofa (`cinema.js`). Its programme is race films (films made for Black audiences, most of them by Black filmmakers) and other early Black cinema in the U.S. public domain. U.S. copyright on works published before 1931 has run out; the two later films are free for the reasons given with them. The notes in the programme are short and factual.

Every film plays from Wikimedia Commons, whose file server (upload.wikimedia.org) sends CORS headers, so the picture can be painted onto the 3D screen. A file's address there is fixed by its name: MediaWiki files it under the first one and first two hex digits of the MD5 of the name (spaces as underscores). Where Commons holds only a 1080p master of several gigabytes, the room first asks for the 480p VP9 copy Commons makes of every video (`…/transcoded/<h>/<hh>/<name>/<name>.480p.vp9.webm`) and falls back to the master, or to a smaller upload of the same film. The file names below were confirmed from the Commons file pages as they appear in search results; the room was built where archive.org and Wikimedia could not be reached, so the files themselves were not fetched, and the 480p copies in particular are named by Commons' scheme rather than seen.

**Your own links.** The cinema also plays a link you paste. A direct video file (.mp4, .webm) plays on the screen. If its server sends CORS headers, each frame is copied onto the screen; otherwise, and in Safari, the video itself is laid over the room and fitted to the screen in perspective. A YouTube link plays in a window, in YouTube's privacy-enhanced player (youtube-nocookie.com); the screen behind it shows a title card, since an embedded player cannot be painted onto a 3D surface. A shared room carries only the link (for YouTube, the video's id), never the video, and checks it: only http and https links, and only well-formed YouTube ids. What you choose to play is your own responsibility.
